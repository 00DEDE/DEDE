Our Four
Worlds

UNESCO's mission spans four interconnected dimensions of human heritage. Each represents a world of meaning — built, natural, intangible, and linguistic — shaped across centuries and sustained by communities worldwide. Together, they form the inheritance we are called to safeguard.

The World Built Through Us

972 Cultural Sites Protected

Throughout continents and centuries, humanity has shaped material into memory. Rising from mountains, deserts, and oceans, these structures reveal how cultures transform landscape into meaning and leave lasting forms woven into the shared story of humanity.

Machu Picchu

Cusco Region, Peru · UNESCO 1983

High in the Andes Mountains, this fifteenth-century Inca city was integrated into steep mountain ridges using agricultural terraces and precisely carved ashlar masonry that required no mortar.

Joya de Cerén

La Libertad, El Salvador · UNESCO 1993

Around 600 CE, a volcanic eruption buried this Maya farming village in ash, perfectly preserving homes, tools, crops, and food — revealing how ordinary people lived and worked in remarkable detail.

Borobudur

Central Java, Indonesia · UNESCO 1991

The largest Buddhist monument ever constructed, this ninth-century temple features nine terraces of intricate relief carvings and hundreds of bell-shaped stupas symbolizing the path toward enlightenment.

Petra

Ma'an Governorate, Jordan · UNESCO 1985

Founded over two thousand years ago by the Nabataeans, this city features monumental structures carved directly into rose-colored sandstone cliffs along ancient trade routes linking Arabia, Egypt, and the Mediterranean.

Timbuktu

Mali, West Africa · UNESCO 1988

At the edge of the Sahara, this medieval city flourished as a center of trans-Saharan trade and Islamic scholarship, housing famous mosques and thousands of handwritten manuscripts on science, law, and literature.

Nan Madol

Pohnpei, Micronesia · UNESCO 2016

Built upon a network of artificial islets connected by canals, this twelfth-century ceremonial city was constructed from massive basalt columns transported without metal tools or wheeled transport.

The World That Sustains Us

235 Natural Sites Preserved

Before monuments, our planet composed its own masterpieces. Coral reefs, savannas, forests, and canyons sustain life while also reminding us that humanity should and will always exist within nature's vast living design.

Great Barrier Reef

Queensland, Australia · UNESCO 1981

The largest living structure on Earth, this coral ecosystem stretches over 2,300 kilometres along Australia's coast, sheltering six of the world's seven sea turtle species and countless marine organisms.

Serengeti National Park

Northern Tanzania · UNESCO 1981

Each year, millions of wildebeest, zebras, and gazelles migrate across these vast savannas in the largest terrestrial wildlife movement on Earth, followed by predators who depend on the same cycle of life.

Socotra Archipelago

Arabian Sea, Yemen · UNESCO 2008

Isolated for millions of years, these islands evolved species found nowhere else on Earth, including the iconic dragon's blood tree and bottle tree that create an almost otherworldly landscape.

Wulingyuan Scenic Area

Hunan Province, China · UNESCO 1992

Thousands of towering quartz sandstone pillars rise from forested valleys, sculpted over millions of years by wind and water into narrow spires, bridges, and mist-shrouded ravines.

Lake Turkana National Park

Northern Kenya · UNESCO 1997

The largest permanent desert lake in the world, surrounded by volcanic formations and containing fossil sites that have revealed crucial chapters in human evolution, including ancient hominid remains.

Grand Canyon National Park

Arizona, United States · UNESCO 1979

Carved over millions of years by the Colorado River, the canyon's exposed rock layers record nearly two billion years of Earth's geological history across 446 kilometres of dramatic cliffs and plateaus.

The World That Moves Within Us

849 Living Traditions Safeguarded

Not all heritage stands in stone. Much of it lives within movement, rhythm, and craft. These living traditions travel across generations, reminding us that culture survives wherever humanity gathers to create, perform, and share collective memory.

Flamenco

Andalusia, Spain · UNESCO 2010

Rooted in Andalusian communities where Roma, Moorish, Jewish, and Spanish cultures converged, this tradition blends rhythmic footwork, expressive singing, and intricate guitar playing with an emotional intensity known as duende.

Jazz

New Orleans, United States · UNESCO 2011

Born in African American communities of early twentieth-century New Orleans, jazz placed improvisation at its core, blending blues, spirituals, and ragtime into a musical language that shaped genres from swing and bebop to hip-hop.

Backstrap Loom Weaving

Guatemalan Highlands

For thousands of years, Maya artisans have used portable looms attached to their bodies to weave vibrant textiles with patterns representing mountains, animals, and cosmological beliefs.

Tango

Buenos Aires & Montevideo · UNESCO 2009

Emerging from immigrant communities in the nineteenth century, tango fused European, African, and local musical traditions around the distinctive sound of the bandoneón and dramatic, close-partnered dance.

The World That Remembers Us

2,500+ Endangered Languages

Language is humanity's most delicate inheritance. Through voices and symbols, cultures preserve identity and lived experience. Each word spoken becomes an act of continuity, ensuring that the voices of communities endure across time and generations.

Ōlelo Hawaiʻi

Hawaii, United States

Nearly silenced by colonial policies, the Hawaiian language was revived through immersion schools and cultural programs, restoring the oral traditions, chants, and genealogies that connect Native Hawaiians to their land and ancestors.

Nüshu Script

Hunan Province, China · UNESCO 2009

The only known writing system created and used exclusively by women, this delicate script allowed women in rural China to communicate through letters, poems, and songs embroidered into textiles and shared during gatherings.

Occitan

Southern France, Northern Spain & Italy

Once the language of troubadour poets who shaped medieval European literature in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Occitan survives through regional communities and cultural associations across southern Europe.

Ainu

Hokkaido, Japan

The language of the Indigenous Ainu people carries ecological knowledge and spiritual traditions through epic songs known as yukar, reflecting centuries of deep interaction with Japan's northern landscapes.

Four dimensions of heritage that together form the complete tapestry of human civilization.
